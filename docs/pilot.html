Can you prove what your agents did?

This is for organizations that have already put AI agents in front of data they care about — and have discovered that “the agent said it did X” is not an answer a security team, an auditor, or a regulator will accept.

Who this is for

You have agents (or are about to) touching documents, tickets, code, or customer records. You have a policy about what they may do. What you do not have is a mechanism that enforces that policy at the action, and a record that proves what happened — independent of the model’s own account of itself.

Good fits: regulated engineering teams, security teams with an agent rollout stuck in review, platform teams who have been asked “how do you know?”.

The problem, stated plainly

Today’s agent security is guardrails: classifiers that read prompts and flag things. They are advisory. When they are wrong, nothing stops the action — and when they are right, they leave a log line, not evidence.

  1. Authority leaks through the model. If the agent can be talked into requesting something, and the request path trusts the agent, the model is the authority. It should never be.
  2. Data leaves through allowed channels. The destination was permitted, the credentials were valid, and the bytes were the attack.
  3. The record is a narrative. Application logs say what the agent claims. They are not signed, not chained, and not anchored anywhere the attacker cannot rewrite.

AISL is built on the opposite premise: the model is not the authority, the infrastructure is.

The deliverable is not a demo. It is an evidence bundle you can hand to someone who was not in the room: every action, the authority that permitted it, and a hash chain that proves nothing was rewritten afterwards.

What AISL enforces during the pilot

  • Capability-based authority. Every action carries a signed capability naming the resource, action, classification ceiling, and expiry. The model cannot mint one; delegation is attenuation-checked against the parent.
  • One enforcement path. Nothing executes unless both a capability and the policy allow it.
  • Containment and egress control. Taint follows data across a session; egress is classified, canary-scanned, and byte-capped before it leaves.
  • Evidence. Hash-chained, per-event signed, externally anchored, with operator-held checkpoints and recorded legal holds.
  • Fail-closed with a loud health signal — a distinct reason and an operator-visible transition, never a silent degradation.

What it does not do — read this first

  • It does not contain a hostile host. The sandbox is in-process. If an attacker owns the machine, they own the keys. Real isolation (microVM, HSM, attestation) is not shipped.
  • It does not judge intent. A model doing something harmful inside granted scope is out of reach. AISL constrains authority, not meaning.
  • It does not replace your DLP, SIEM, or IdP. It produces evidence; it is not a dashboard, a SOC, or a certificate.
  • It is pre-1.0. Sharp edges, and direct access to the person who fixes them.
